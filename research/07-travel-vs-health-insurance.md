# Travel Insurance vs. Health Insurance for International Students

## Why This Matters

Every year, thousands of international students arrive in the U.S. believing they have health insurance -- only to discover they actually have travel insurance. The two sound similar and are often sold by the same companies. But when a student gets sick, the differences can mean tens of thousands of dollars in unexpected bills.

---

## At a Glance: Travel Insurance vs. Health Insurance

| Feature | Travel Insurance / Travel Medical | Comprehensive Health Insurance |
|---|---|---|
| **Designed for** | Travelers on short trips abroad | People living outside their home country |
| **Typical duration** | Days to a few months | Academic year or longer (renewable) |
| **Pre-existing conditions** | Usually excluded entirely | Often covered (with waiting periods) |
| **Preventive care** | Not covered | Covered (annual checkups, vaccines) |
| **Mental health** | Not covered or extremely limited | Covered (therapy, psychiatry) |
| **Prescription drugs** | Emergency only, if at all | Ongoing prescriptions covered |
| **Maternity** | Excluded | Often covered |
| **Claim structure** | Fixed/scheduled dollar amounts per service | Percentage of actual charges (e.g., 80/20) |
| **Maximum benefit** | $50,000 - $250,000 typical | $500,000 - $5,000,000+ |
| **Network** | Usually none (or limited PPO access) | PPO or HMO network with negotiated rates |
| **University SHIP waiver** | Almost never accepted | Accepted if it meets waiver criteria |
| **ACA compliant** | No | Some plans are; university SHIPs always are |
| **Trip cancellation/baggage** | Often included | Not included (not relevant) |

Sources: Air-Dr.com, "Travel Insurance vs Health Insurance: Key Differences"; Shorelight, "International Student Health Insurance in the USA: A 2026 Guide"; AmericanVisitorInsurance.com, "Fixed Benefits vs Comprehensive Travel Insurance."

## What Travel Insurance Actually Covers

Travel insurance -- sometimes marketed as "travel medical insurance" -- is designed for short-term travelers, not residents. Its core purpose is to handle unexpected emergencies that happen during a trip. Typical coverage includes:

- **Emergency medical treatment** for accidents or sudden illness
- **Emergency medical evacuation** to the nearest adequate facility or home country
- **Repatriation of remains** if the insured dies abroad
- **Trip cancellation and interruption** (lost flights, weather delays)
- **Lost or delayed baggage**
- **Emergency dental** (injury only, not routine care)

The key word is "emergency." Travel insurance will pay for a broken arm from a fall or an ER visit for sudden appendicitis. It will not pay for ongoing treatment, routine care, or anything that existed before the trip started.

## What Health Insurance Covers

Comprehensive health insurance for international students functions like the health insurance a U.S. resident would carry. It covers:

- **Doctor visits** (primary care, specialists)
- **Hospitalization and surgery**
- **Preventive care** (annual physicals, immunizations, screenings)
- **Mental health services** (therapy, psychiatry, crisis counseling)
- **Prescription medications** (ongoing, not just emergency)
- **Lab work and diagnostic imaging**
- **Maternity care** (often with waiting periods)
- **Pre-existing conditions** (typically after a waiting period of 6-12 months)
- **Chronic disease management** (diabetes, asthma, etc.)
- **Urgent care and ER visits**

The policy duration matches the academic year and is renewable, so students maintain continuous coverage.

## Why International Students Confuse the Two

Several factors create this confusion:

1. **Marketing language is deliberately vague.** Companies like ISO, IMG, and WorldTrips sell both travel medical and comprehensive plans. Their websites list plans side-by-side, and some plan names include words like "student" or "scholar" even when the product is a travel medical plan, not comprehensive coverage.

2. **Parents buy insurance before departure.** In many countries (especially China, India, South Korea), parents purchase insurance for their children before they leave. They search for "international student insurance" and find travel medical plans that are cheaper than comprehensive ones. The lower price looks attractive, and the family may not understand the difference.

3. **Home-country insurance norms differ.** In countries with universal healthcare, the concept of needing separate health insurance is unfamiliar. A parent may think "insurance for traveling abroad" and "insurance for living abroad" are the same thing.

4. **Short-term plans are easy to buy online.** Travel medical insurance can be purchased in minutes with a credit card. Comprehensive plans often require enrollment through a university or a more complex application process.

5. **Some plans are hybrids.** Products like WorldTrips StudentSecure offer tiered plans (Budget, Smart, Select, Elite) where lower tiers resemble travel medical insurance and higher tiers approach comprehensive coverage. This makes it even harder to tell what you are actually buying.

## Key Coverage Gaps in Travel Insurance

### Pre-existing Conditions

This is the most dangerous gap. Travel insurance almost universally excludes pre-existing conditions -- any medical condition diagnosed, treated, or for which symptoms existed before the policy start date. This means:

- A student with asthma who has an asthma attack: **not covered**
- A student on medication for depression who needs a refill: **not covered**
- A student with a known heart condition who has a cardiac event: **not covered**

Some comprehensive plans also have pre-existing condition exclusions, but they typically include a "look-back period" (e.g., 6 or 12 months) after which pre-existing conditions become covered.

### Ongoing and Chronic Care

Travel insurance covers acute, unexpected events -- not ongoing management of chronic conditions. A student with Type 1 diabetes needs insulin, test strips, endocrinologist visits, and lab work throughout the year. Travel insurance covers none of this.

### Mental Health

Most travel medical plans either exclude mental health entirely or cap it at a very low amount (e.g., $1,000 - $5,000). For international students facing culture shock, academic pressure, isolation, and language barriers, mental health coverage is not optional -- it is essential. Comprehensive plans typically cover therapy and psychiatry visits with standard copays.

### Preventive Care

Travel insurance does not cover annual checkups, vaccinations (often required by universities), routine bloodwork, or health screenings. Many universities require proof of immunizations like MMR, meningitis, and TB testing. Without preventive care coverage, students pay out of pocket.

### Prescription Drugs

Travel insurance may cover a short course of medication prescribed during an emergency visit, but it does not cover ongoing prescriptions. A student who needs daily medication for ADHD, anxiety, allergies, or any chronic condition will not be covered.

### Maternity

Travel insurance almost always excludes pregnancy and maternity care entirely. Comprehensive plans may cover maternity after a waiting period.

## "Scheduled Benefit" Plans vs. "Comprehensive" Plans

This is one of the most important distinctions and one of the least understood.

### Scheduled Benefit (Fixed Benefit) Plans

A scheduled benefit plan pays a **pre-set dollar amount** for each type of medical service, regardless of the actual cost. The insurance company publishes a schedule -- a table listing the maximum they will pay for each procedure.

**Example schedule:**
| Service | Plan Pays (Fixed Amount) | Typical Actual Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Hospital room (per day) | $1,500 | $3,000 - $10,000+ |
| Surgery | $3,000 | $15,000 - $100,000+ |
| ER visit | $500 | $2,000 - $5,000+ |
| Doctor office visit | $100 | $200 - $400 |
| X-ray | $75 | $200 - $500 |
| MRI | $400 | $1,500 - $3,000 |
| Prescription (per fill) | $25 | $50 - $500+ |

The student pays the difference between the scheduled amount and the actual bill. On a $50,000 surgery, a plan that pays $3,000 leaves the student with a $47,000 bill.

Scheduled benefit plans are best suited for healthy travelers on short trips who want the lowest possible premium and accept the risk of large out-of-pocket costs.

### Comprehensive Plans

A comprehensive plan pays a **percentage of actual covered charges** (typically 80% after deductible, with the insured paying 20% coinsurance up to an out-of-pocket maximum). These plans function like standard U.S. health insurance:

- Deductible: $100 - $500
- Coinsurance: 80/20 or 90/10
- Out-of-pocket maximum: $5,000 - $10,000
- Maximum benefit: $500,000 - $5,000,000+
- Use a PPO network with negotiated rates

On that same $50,000 surgery, a comprehensive plan with a $250 deductible and 80/20 coinsurance would pay roughly $39,800, leaving the student with approximately $10,200 -- and often less, if an out-of-pocket maximum applies.

Sources: Squaremouth, "Scheduled Benefit vs. Comprehensive Travel Insurance"; AmericanVisitorInsurance.com, "Fixed Benefits vs Comprehensive Visitor Insurance."

## How Claim Limits Work

### Travel Insurance Claim Limits

Travel insurance policies typically have:

- **Per-incident limits**: $50,000 to $250,000 per accident or illness
- **Sub-limits**: Caps on specific categories (e.g., $500 for prescriptions, $1,000 for mental health, $2,500 for physical therapy)
- **Scheduled amounts**: Fixed dollar payments per service (see above)
- **No out-of-pocket maximum**: The insured's exposure is open-ended because the plan pays fixed amounts, not percentages

A student who needs multiple ER visits, follow-up care, and physical therapy can quickly exhaust sub-limits while the overall policy limit appears adequate on paper.

### Health Insurance Claim Limits

Comprehensive health insurance typically has:

- **Annual or lifetime maximum**: $500,000 to $5,000,000+ (or unlimited for ACA-compliant plans)
- **Out-of-pocket maximum**: A cap on what the insured pays (e.g., $6,000/year), after which the plan pays 100%
- **No sub-limits** on most covered services
- **Negotiated network rates**: The plan pays based on discounted rates, not billed charges

The out-of-pocket maximum is the critical difference. It means a student's financial exposure has a ceiling, no matter how expensive the treatment.

## What Happens When a Student with Travel Insurance Goes to the ER

Here is a realistic scenario:

> A student with a fixed-benefit travel insurance plan ($150,000 maximum) slips on ice and breaks their wrist. They go to the ER.

**Typical ER costs:**
- ER facility fee: $2,500
- X-rays: $400
- Orthopedic consultation: $600
- Cast/splint: $300
- Prescription painkillers: $50
- Follow-up visit (1 week): $250
- **Total: ~$4,100**

**What the scheduled benefit plan pays:**
- ER visit: $500
- X-rays: $75
- Physician: $100
- Supplies: $50
- Prescription: $25
- Follow-up: $100
- **Total paid: ~$850**

**Student's out-of-pocket cost: ~$3,250** -- for a broken wrist.

With a comprehensive plan (80/20, $250 deductible), the student would pay approximately $1,020 ($250 deductible + 20% of $3,850).

For more serious events -- appendectomy ($30,000+), car accident requiring surgery ($100,000+), or cancer diagnosis ($200,000+) -- the gap between travel insurance payouts and actual costs becomes catastrophic.

## University SHIP Waivers: Travel Insurance Almost Never Accepted

Universities that offer a Student Health Insurance Plan (SHIP) typically allow students to waive enrollment if they can prove they have equivalent coverage. However, travel insurance almost universally fails to meet waiver criteria.

**Specific university policies:**

- **UCLA**: "Travel Plans, Health Care Sharing Ministries (HCSMs), County Medical Service Plans (CMSP), and SIMNSA do not meet waiver criteria and are not eligible to waive UC SHIP enrollment."

- **UC Irvine**: "Travel plans, reimbursement programs from your home country or home government, and plans owned, headquartered or operated outside of the United States will not qualify to be used to waive enrollment in UC IRVINE SHIP."

- **University of Wisconsin-Madison**: "Individual and private insurance plans (including travel insurance and Marketplace plans) DO NOT qualify for a waiver for international students."

- **UT Austin**: Requires waiver-eligible plans to include preventive care, mental health coverage, prescription drug coverage, and no exclusion for pre-existing conditions -- none of which travel insurance provides.

**Why travel insurance fails SHIP waiver requirements:**

1. Does not cover preventive care
2. Excludes or severely limits mental health services
3. Excludes pre-existing conditions
4. Has insufficient maximum benefit limits
5. Uses scheduled/fixed benefits instead of percentage-based coinsurance
6. Is not issued by a U.S.-domiciled insurance company (in some cases)
7. Does not include an out-of-pocket maximum
8. Policy duration does not align with the academic year

Students who arrive with travel insurance expecting to waive SHIP are forced to pay for both -- the travel insurance they already purchased (non-refundable) plus the mandatory SHIP enrollment.

Sources: UCLA Arthur Ashe Student Health; UC Irvine Student Health Center; UW-Madison University Health Services; UT Austin Texas Global.

## J-1 Visa: State Department Insurance Requirements (22 CFR 62.14)

Exchange visitors on J-1 visas must carry insurance that meets specific federal minimums under 22 CFR 62.14. Travel insurance sometimes meets these requirements on paper, but often with gaps.

### Minimum Requirements

| Requirement | Minimum Amount |
|---|---|
| Medical benefits per accident or illness | $100,000 |
| Repatriation of remains | $25,000 |
| Medical evacuation | $50,000 |
| Maximum deductible per accident or illness | $500 |

### Insurance Provider Requirements

The insurer must hold at least one of the following ratings:
- A.M. Best: "A-" or above
- Standard & Poor's Claims-paying Ability: "A-" or above
- Weiss Research: "B+" or above
- Fitch Ratings: "A-" or above
- Demotech: "A" or above

### Where Travel Insurance Falls Short for J-1

Many travel medical plans technically meet the $100,000 medical benefit minimum but fail in practice because:

1. **Scheduled benefit structure**: The plan may have a $100,000 "maximum" but pays only fixed amounts per service. The effective coverage is far less than $100,000.
2. **Pre-existing condition exclusions**: The regulation does not explicitly require pre-existing condition coverage, but a J-1 scholar with a chronic condition is effectively uninsured for their most likely medical needs.
3. **Mental health exclusions**: Not addressed by 22 CFR 62.14, but critical for scholars living abroad for 1-3 years.
4. **No network**: Without negotiated provider rates, the billed charges eat through the $100,000 limit much faster.
5. **Program sponsor requirements**: Many J-1 program sponsors (universities, research institutions) impose requirements **above** the federal minimum, including mental health coverage, preventive care, and higher benefit limits.

A plan can be "22 CFR 62.14 compliant" and still leave a J-1 scholar dangerously underinsured.

Sources: 22 CFR 62.14 via eCFR; MIT International Scholars Office; Princeton Davis International Center; USC Office of International Services; TCU International Services.

## Common Insurance Providers for International Students

### ISO (International Student Organization)

- Operating since 1958; one of the most recognized names in international student insurance
- Offers both travel medical and comprehensive plans
- Website: isoa.org

### IMG (International Medical Group)

- Offers "Student Journey Medical Insurance" and other plans
- Markets plans as meeting J-1 visa requirements; provides both scheduled benefit and comprehensive options
- Website: imglobal.com

### WorldTrips

- Offers **StudentSecure** with four tiers: Budget, Smart, Select, Elite
- Budget is essentially travel medical (lowest premiums, highest risk); Elite approaches comprehensive coverage
- Also offers **Atlas Travel** (travel medical, not student health)
- The tiered structure means students can accidentally buy the cheapest tier thinking all StudentSecure plans are equivalent
- Website: worldtrips.com

**Warning**: All three companies sell products across the spectrum. The company name alone does not indicate coverage type. Students must read the specific plan documents.

Sources: ISOA.org; IMGlobal.com; WorldTrips.com; NerdWallet IMG Travel Insurance Review.

## Red Flags: How to Tell if a Plan Is Travel Insurance, Not Health Insurance

Use this checklist. If a plan has **three or more** of these red flags, it is likely travel insurance or a scheduled benefit plan that will not provide adequate coverage:

- [ ] **"Scheduled benefit" or "fixed benefit" language** anywhere in the plan documents
- [ ] **A benefit schedule table** listing flat dollar amounts per procedure
- [ ] **No out-of-pocket maximum** for the insured
- [ ] **Pre-existing conditions excluded** with no path to future coverage
- [ ] **Mental health not listed** in covered benefits, or capped below $5,000
- [ ] **No preventive care** (no annual checkup, no immunizations)
- [ ] **No prescription drug coverage** for ongoing medications
- [ ] **Trip cancellation or baggage loss** included as benefits (this is travel insurance)
- [ ] **Maximum benefit below $300,000** (many are $50,000 - $100,000)
- [ ] **Policy sold in daily or weekly increments** rather than semester/annual terms
- [ ] **No PPO or HMO network** listed
- [ ] **"Travel medical"** in the plan name or marketing
- [ ] **Premium under $50/month** (comprehensive plans for students typically cost $100-$300/month)
- [ ] **Not ACA-compliant** (though not all legitimate international student plans are ACA-compliant, this is still a signal)
- [ ] **Maternity explicitly excluded**
- [ ] **University is not listed as a partner** or the plan is not on the university's approved waiver list

## The Bottom Line

Travel insurance and health insurance serve fundamentally different purposes. Travel insurance is a safety net for emergencies during a trip. Health insurance is a system for managing all of a person's medical needs while living somewhere.

International students are not travelers. They are residents -- living, studying, and building lives in the U.S. for one to six years. They need health insurance, not travel insurance.

**What students should do:**

1. **Enroll in the university's SHIP** unless they have a confirmed waiver-eligible plan
2. **If buying private insurance**, verify it is a comprehensive plan (not scheduled benefit) by checking the red flag list above
3. **If on a J-1 visa**, confirm the plan meets 22 CFR 62.14 requirements AND any additional requirements from their program sponsor
4. **Never assume** a plan is adequate because it has "student" in the name or is sold by a company that also sells student plans
5. **Read the benefit schedule** -- if it lists fixed dollar amounts per service, it is not comprehensive coverage

The cost difference between travel insurance ($30-60/month) and comprehensive health insurance ($100-300/month) is real. But one ER visit on a scheduled benefit plan can cost more out of pocket than an entire year of comprehensive premiums.
