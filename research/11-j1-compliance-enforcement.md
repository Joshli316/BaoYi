# State Department Compliance Enforcement for J-1 Sponsor Organizations: Health Insurance Requirements

## Overview

The Exchange Visitor Program (J-1 visa) is governed by the Mutual Educational and Cultural Exchange Act of 1961 (Fulbright-Hays Act), as amended, and implemented through regulations codified at **22 CFR Part 62**. The Department of State's Bureau of Educational and Cultural Affairs (ECA) oversees designated sponsor organizations and enforces compliance with all program requirements, including mandatory health insurance coverage for exchange visitors and their J-2 dependents.

Insurance compliance is not optional or advisory. It is a **regulatory mandate** with consequences that can escalate to program termination for participants and designation revocation for sponsors.

---

## Regulatory Foundation: The Minimum Insurance Requirements

Under **22 CFR 62.14**, every J-1 exchange visitor and accompanying J-2 dependent must maintain insurance that meets or exceeds these minimums throughout their entire program:

| Requirement | Minimum Standard |
|---|---|
| Medical benefits | $100,000 per accident or illness |
| Repatriation of remains | $25,000 |
| Medical evacuation | $50,000 |
| Deductible | Not to exceed $500 per accident or illness |
| Underwriter rating | A.M. Best A- or above, Insurance Solvency International rating of A-i or above, Standard and Poor's Claims-Paying Ability rating of A- or above, or Weiss Research Inc. rating of B+ or above |

Key details:

- Coverage must be in effect for the **entire duration** of the exchange visitor's program, including any grace period.
- The insurance may be provided through the sponsor, the host organization, the participant's home country, or purchased privately -- but it **must meet the regulatory minimums** regardless of source.
- Plans with "per condition" caps, restrictive pre-existing condition exclusions that effectively negate the $100,000 minimum, or sub-rated underwriters do not satisfy the regulation.

## How Designated Sponsors Verify Insurance Compliance

### Initial Verification

Sponsors are required to verify that participants have compliant insurance **before or at the time of program start**. Standard practices include:

- **Requiring proof of insurance** as part of the DS-2019 issuance or pre-arrival documentation packet.
- **Offering or mandating a sponsor-selected group plan** that is pre-verified to meet 22 CFR 62.14 standards. Many sponsors auto-enroll participants and include the premium in program fees.
- **Reviewing policy documents** to confirm coverage amounts, deductible limits, underwriter ratings, and that repatriation/evacuation benefits are included.
- **Using third-party compliance verification services** (e.g., Envisage International's verification tool, or insurance brokers specializing in J-1 plans) to confirm plan compliance.

### Ongoing Verification

Sponsors must verify insurance compliance is maintained throughout the program. Common approaches:

| Verification Method | Frequency | Used By |
|---|---|---|
| Auto-enrollment in sponsor group plan | Continuous (no gaps possible) | Large sponsors, au pair agencies, internship sponsors |
| Periodic self-certification by participant | Monthly, quarterly, or semi-annually | University sponsors, smaller organizations |
| Proof of coverage submission (upload policy docs) | At check-in intervals | Work & travel sponsors, cultural exchange programs |
| Automated renewal tracking | Monthly or per policy term | Sponsors using insurance management platforms |
| In-person orientation verification | At arrival/orientation | Summer work & travel, camp counselor programs |

## Enforcement Process: What Happens When a Participant Lacks Coverage

### Step 1: Discovery

Non-compliance is typically discovered through:

- Routine sponsor audits or check-ins
- Participant self-reporting (e.g., policy lapsed, employer coverage ended)
- Medical incident where provider discovers inadequate or absent coverage
- Department of State compliance review or site visit

### Step 2: Notification and Grace Period

**22 CFR 62.14(h)** provides a specific enforcement timeline:

> If a sponsor learns that an exchange visitor or accompanying spouse or dependent does not have the required insurance, the sponsor must **inform the exchange visitor** of the requirement and give the visitor **30 days** to obtain compliant coverage.

This 30-day window is a **regulatory grace period**, not a discretionary sponsor decision. The clock starts when the sponsor becomes aware of the lapse.

### Step 3: End Program if Non-Compliance Continues

If the exchange visitor **fails to obtain compliant insurance within 30 days**, the sponsor is required to **end the visitor's program**. Per 22 CFR 62.14(h):

> If, within 30 days, the exchange visitor has not obtained the required insurance, the sponsor must **terminate** the exchange visitor's program.

This is not discretionary. The regulation uses mandatory language ("must terminate").

### Step 4: SEVIS Record Update

Upon program termination for insurance non-compliance:

- The sponsor updates the participant's SEVIS record to reflect termination.
- The participant loses valid J-1 status.
- The participant is expected to depart the United States or apply for a change of status if eligible.

### Summary Enforcement Timeline

| Day | Action |
|---|---|
| Day 0 | Sponsor discovers insurance lapse |
| Day 0-1 | Sponsor notifies participant of the requirement and 30-day deadline |
| Day 1-30 | Participant must obtain compliant coverage |
| Day 30 | If no compliant coverage: sponsor **must** terminate the program |
| Post-termination | SEVIS record updated; participant must depart the U.S. |

## How Sponsors Handle Mid-Program Coverage Loss

Common scenarios that cause mid-program coverage gaps:

- **Employer-provided insurance changes or ends** (e.g., participant changes host employer, job ends early)
- **Participant fails to renew** a monthly or term-based plan
- **Home-country plan lapses** or is discovered to not actually meet minimums
- **Participant switches from compliant to non-compliant plan** (e.g., marketplace plan with a $2,000 deductible)

### Sponsor response protocol:

1. **Immediate notification** to the participant explaining the lapse and the 30-day regulatory deadline.
2. **Offer alternatives**: most sponsors have a designated fallback plan participants can enroll in immediately. Many sponsors maintain relationships with J-1 specialty insurers (ISO, Envisage, GeoBlue, IMG, Seven Corners) for this purpose.
3. **Document the notification and deadline** in the participant's file.
4. **Follow up** before the 30-day deadline expires.
5. **Terminate if necessary** and update SEVIS.

Best-practice sponsors proactively prevent gaps by:

- Sending automated reminders before policy expiration dates
- Requiring proof of renewal before the prior policy term ends
- Auto-enrolling participants in sponsor plans with continuous coverage

## Consequences for the Sponsor Organization

### Regulatory Risk

If the Department of State determines that a sponsor has **systematically failed** to verify or enforce insurance compliance, consequences include:

| Consequence | Severity | Trigger |
|---|---|---|
| Letter of concern | Low | Minor or isolated lapses discovered during review |
| Probation / increased reporting requirements | Medium | Pattern of non-compliance across multiple participants |
| Sanctions (limitation on program size, categories, or operations) | High | Repeated or willful failure to enforce requirements |
| Designation revocation | Maximum | Egregious, systematic, or willful non-compliance; failure to cooperate with DoS oversight |

Under **22 CFR 62.50**, the Department of State may sanction, suspend, or revoke a sponsor's designation for failure to comply with any provision of the regulations, including 22 CFR 62.14.

### Practical Risk

- **Liability exposure**: If an uninsured participant has a serious medical event, the sponsor may face lawsuits, negative publicity, and government scrutiny.
- **Reputational damage**: Designation actions are publicly available and can destroy a sponsor's ability to recruit participants and partner with host organizations.
- **Increased oversight**: Sponsors placed on probation face more frequent DoS compliance reviews, additional reporting requirements, and restrictions on program growth.

## Department of State's Role in Enforcement

### Bureau of Educational and Cultural Affairs (ECA)

ECA's Office of Designation oversees the approximately 1,400 designated J-1 sponsor organizations. Its enforcement activities include:

- **Biennial compliance reviews**: Every designated sponsor must submit a biennial report (Form DS-3036 and supporting documentation) demonstrating ongoing compliance with all regulatory requirements, including insurance.
- **Site visits and audits**: ECA conducts both scheduled and unannounced site visits to sponsor offices and host organization locations. Auditors may review participant files, insurance documentation, and compliance procedures.
- **Complaint investigations**: ECA investigates complaints from participants, host organizations, and third parties regarding sponsor non-compliance.
- **SEVIS data analysis**: ECA monitors aggregate SEVIS data for patterns that may indicate compliance problems (e.g., unusually high termination rates, clusters of participants without documented insurance).

### Office of Inspector General (OIG)

The State Department OIG has conducted periodic reviews of the Exchange Visitor Program, including assessments of sponsor compliance enforcement. OIG reports have historically identified weaknesses in:

- Sponsor self-reporting reliability
- ECA's capacity to conduct sufficient site visits given the number of sponsors
- Documentation standards for insurance verification

### Compliance Review Cycle

| Activity | Frequency |
|---|---|
| Sponsor biennial report (DS-3036) | Every 2 years |
| Routine ECA site visits | Risk-based; typically every 2-5 years per sponsor |
| Complaint-driven investigations | As needed |
| SEVIS data monitoring | Ongoing |
| OIG program reviews | Periodic (not on fixed schedule) |

## How Different Sponsor Types Handle Compliance

### Universities and Colleges (J-1 Scholar, Student, Short-Term Scholar)

- Typically have established international offices with dedicated compliance staff.
- Often mandate enrollment in the university's health insurance plan, which is pre-verified for J-1 compliance.
- Waiver processes exist for scholars with qualifying external coverage, but require documented review against 22 CFR 62.14 standards.
- Compliance tracking is usually integrated with the university's international student/scholar management system.

### Au Pair Sponsors

- Highly regulated sub-category with additional requirements under 22 CFR 62.31.
- Sponsors typically auto-enroll all participants in a group plan and include the cost in program fees.
- Insurance gaps are rare because coverage is continuous and sponsor-managed.
- Local coordinators conduct monthly in-person contact, providing additional compliance touchpoints.

### Summer Work & Travel Sponsors

- High-volume programs (tens of thousands of participants annually) with higher compliance risk due to:
  - Geographic dispersion of participants across the U.S.
  - Participants arranging their own host employers
  - Short program duration (typically 3-4 months) with frequent turnover
- Many sponsors require enrollment in a sponsor-selected plan at program start.
- Some allow participants to use employer-provided coverage but require proof of compliance.
- This category has historically received the most ECA scrutiny and enforcement attention.

### Intern and Trainee Sponsors

- Host organizations sometimes provide coverage, but sponsors must verify it meets J-1 minimums.
- Sponsors typically require insurance documentation before issuing the DS-2019 and at periodic check-ins.
- For longer programs (up to 12 or 18 months), ongoing verification is critical.

### Government and Research Sponsors (Fulbright, NIH, etc.)

- Government-sponsored programs generally provide compliant insurance as part of the program.
- Compliance risk is lowest in this category due to centralized administration.

## Documentation Sponsors Must Maintain

Per 22 CFR 62.10 and related provisions, sponsors must maintain records that demonstrate compliance. For insurance specifically:

- **Proof of insurance coverage** for each participant and J-2 dependent (policy documents, certificates of coverage, enrollment confirmations).
- **Evidence that coverage meets 22 CFR 62.14 minimums** (coverage amounts, deductible, underwriter rating).
- **Records of compliance notifications** sent to participants who were found non-compliant.
- **Documentation of the 30-day cure period** and its outcome (participant obtained coverage or program was terminated).
- **Termination records** including the reason (insurance non-compliance) in SEVIS.

Records must be maintained for **at least 3 years** following the end of the exchange visitor's program, and must be available for inspection by the Department of State upon request.

## SEVIS and Insurance Compliance Tracking

### What SEVIS Does

The Student and Exchange Visitor Information System (SEVIS) is the primary database for tracking J-1 exchange visitors. Sponsors use SEVIS to:

- Issue and manage DS-2019 forms
- Record program start, extension, transfer, and termination
- Update participant status and employment information
- Document terminations, including those for insurance non-compliance

### What SEVIS Does Not Do

SEVIS does **not** have a dedicated insurance compliance tracking module. Specifically:

- There is no field in SEVIS to upload or record insurance policy details.
- SEVIS does not automatically verify whether a participant has compliant insurance.
- Insurance compliance is not flagged or monitored through SEVIS alerts.

### The Gap

This means insurance compliance tracking is **entirely the sponsor's responsibility** using their own internal systems. SEVIS reflects the *outcome* (e.g., program termination for non-compliance) but not the ongoing monitoring process.

Some sponsors use third-party compliance platforms or custom databases to track insurance status, policy expiration dates, and compliance verification. Others rely on spreadsheets or manual processes -- which increases the risk of gaps going undetected.

## Recent Developments and Policy Context

### Rulemaking and Proposed Changes

- The Department of State has periodically considered updating the minimum insurance requirements under 22 CFR 62.14, particularly the $100,000 medical benefit floor and $500 deductible cap, which have not been adjusted in many years despite rising healthcare costs. As of early 2026, no final rule has been published to change these minimums.
- ECA has emphasized sponsor accountability in designation renewal processes, with increased attention to documentation of insurance compliance procedures.

### Enforcement Trends

- ECA has increasingly used designation renewal as a compliance checkpoint, requiring sponsors to demonstrate robust insurance verification procedures as a condition of continued designation.
- Several sponsors have faced designation sanctions or non-renewal in recent years, though ECA does not routinely publish detailed enforcement action summaries. Actions are typically communicated directly to sponsors.
- The OIG's periodic reviews have consistently recommended that ECA strengthen its oversight capacity and standardize compliance verification procedures across sponsor categories.

### Industry Responses

- Major J-1 insurance providers have developed compliance verification tools and certificates specifically designed to help sponsors demonstrate that plans meet 22 CFR 62.14 requirements.
- Sponsor industry groups (such as the Alliance for International Exchange) have advocated for clearer guidance on acceptable verification methods and have developed best-practice recommendations for members.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Insurance is mandatory, not optional.** 22 CFR 62.14 sets specific minimums that must be met throughout the program.
2. **The 30-day cure period is regulatory.** Sponsors must give participants exactly 30 days to obtain coverage after discovering a lapse. If the participant fails, the sponsor must terminate the program.
3. **Sponsors bear the enforcement burden.** SEVIS does not track insurance compliance directly; sponsors must maintain their own verification systems and documentation.
4. **Consequences escalate for sponsors.** Systematic failure to enforce insurance requirements can result in sanctions, probation, or loss of designation.
5. **Different sponsor categories face different risk profiles.** High-volume, dispersed programs (Summer Work & Travel) carry the most compliance risk; centralized programs (au pair, government-sponsored) carry the least.
6. **Documentation is critical.** Sponsors must maintain proof of coverage, compliance notifications, and termination records for at least 3 years.
