# Reading Medical Bills and EOBs in the US Healthcare System

## What Is an EOB (Explanation of Benefits)?

An **Explanation of Benefits (EOB)** is a statement your health insurance company sends you after a healthcare provider files a claim for services you received. It explains:

- What services were provided and when
- How much the provider charged
- How much your insurance plan covered
- How much you may owe

**An EOB is NOT a bill.** Do not pay anything based on your EOB alone. It is an informational document. You will receive a separate bill from the provider for any amount you owe.

### EOB vs. Medical Bill

| Feature | EOB | Medical Bill |
|---|---|---|
| **Sent by** | Your insurance company | The healthcare provider (hospital, doctor, lab) |
| **Purpose** | Explains what insurance covered | Requests payment from you |
| **Action required** | Review for accuracy | Pay the amount owed |
| **Payment due** | No payment due | Has a due date |
| **Arrives** | Usually 1-4 weeks after the visit | Usually 4-8 weeks after the visit (after insurance processes the claim) |

**Key rule:** Always wait for both your EOB and your bill before paying. Compare them to make sure they match.

---

## Key Fields on an EOB

Every EOB contains the same core fields, though formatting varies by insurer.

| Field | What It Means |
|---|---|
| **Date of Service** | The date you received care |
| **Provider Name** | The doctor, hospital, or lab that treated you |
| **Service Description / CPT Code** | What procedure or service was performed (e.g., "Emergency Room Visit," "X-Ray, Chest") |
| **Billed Amount (Provider Charges)** | The full price the provider charged -- this is the "sticker price" before any discounts |
| **Allowed Amount** | The maximum amount your insurance has agreed to pay for that service (the negotiated rate) |
| **Plan Paid (Insurance Paid)** | How much your insurance company actually paid the provider |
| **Deductible Applied** | How much of this charge was applied to your annual deductible |
| **Copay** | Your fixed fee for this type of visit (e.g., $30 for a specialist) |
| **Coinsurance** | Your percentage share after deductible is met (e.g., you pay 20%, plan pays 80%) |
| **Patient Responsibility (You Owe)** | The total amount you are expected to pay -- this should match your eventual bill |
| **Claim Status** | Whether the claim was approved, denied, or pending |
| **Remark/Denial Codes** | Codes explaining why something was reduced or denied |

---

## Billed Amount vs. Allowed Amount vs. Patient Responsibility

These three numbers are the most important -- and most confusing -- on any EOB.

### Billed Amount (Chargemaster Price)
The full price the provider lists for a service. This number is almost always inflated well above what anyone actually pays. Hospitals set these rates using an internal price list called a **chargemaster**. Think of it as the "retail sticker price" that almost nobody pays.

### Allowed Amount (Negotiated Rate)
The maximum amount your insurance company has agreed to pay for that service, based on its contract with the provider. This is always lower than the billed amount -- often 40-60% lower. If the provider is **in-network**, they have agreed to accept the allowed amount as full payment. The difference between the billed amount and the allowed amount is written off.

### Patient Responsibility
After insurance applies the allowed amount and pays its share, the remainder is what you owe. Patient responsibility is made up of:

- **Deductible**: The amount you must pay out of pocket before insurance starts paying (e.g., $1,500/year)
- **Copay**: A flat fee per visit type (e.g., $30 for primary care, $250 for ER)
- **Coinsurance**: A percentage split after your deductible is met (e.g., you pay 20%)

**Example breakdown:**

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| Billed Amount | $5,000 |
| Allowed Amount | $2,800 |
| Provider Write-Off (Billed - Allowed) | -$2,200 |
| Insurance Pays (80% of Allowed) | -$2,240 |
| **Your Responsibility (20% Coinsurance)** | **$560** |

## How to Read a Medical Bill Line by Line

When you receive a medical bill from a provider, check each line:

1. **Patient information** -- Verify your name, address, insurance ID, and date of birth are correct. Errors here can mean the claim was filed wrong.

2. **Service date(s)** -- Confirm these match when you actually received care.

3. **Description of services** -- Each service should have a CPT code (a 5-digit number) and a plain-language description. Look for services you do not recognize.

4. **Charges per line item** -- Each service is listed separately. An ER visit might show 5-10 separate line items (facility fee, physician fee, lab work, imaging, supplies, etc.).

5. **Insurance adjustments** -- The amount your insurance negotiated off (billed amount minus allowed amount).

6. **Insurance payment** -- What your plan paid.

7. **Amount due** -- What you owe. This should match the "Patient Responsibility" on your EOB.

8. **Due date and payment options** -- When payment is due and how to pay.

**Red flag:** If the bill shows no insurance adjustment and charges you the full billed amount, your claim may not have been processed. Call your insurer before paying.

## Balance Billing

**Balance billing** happens when a provider bills you for the difference between their billed amount and the insurance-allowed amount.

**Example:** A doctor charges $1,000. Your insurance allowed amount is $600 and pays $480 (80%). Normally you would owe $120 (your 20%). But with balance billing, the doctor also bills you for the remaining $400 ($1,000 - $600), so you get a bill for $520.

### When Balance Billing Can Happen

- **Out-of-network providers**: If you choose to see a provider who is not in your insurance network, they may balance bill you because they have no contract limiting their charges.
- **Emergency situations**: You may be treated by out-of-network doctors at an in-network hospital (e.g., an out-of-network anesthesiologist during surgery). This was the most common scenario before the No Surprises Act.
- **Ground ambulance**: Currently not covered by federal surprise billing protections (though some states have their own rules).

### When Balance Billing is Illegal

- **In-network providers**: They have agreed to accept the allowed amount. Balance billing by in-network providers is always a billing error.
- **Situations covered by the No Surprises Act** (see below).

---

## No Surprises Act (2022) Protections

The **No Surprises Act** took effect January 1, 2022, and provides federal protections against surprise medical bills. Key protections:

### What It Covers

1. **Emergency services**: You cannot be balance billed for emergency care, even if the hospital or doctors are out-of-network. You pay only your in-network cost-sharing amount (copay, coinsurance, deductible).

2. **Out-of-network providers at in-network facilities**: If you go to an in-network hospital but are treated by an out-of-network doctor (anesthesiologist, radiologist, pathologist, etc.), you are protected from balance billing.

3. **Air ambulance services**: Out-of-network air ambulance providers cannot balance bill you. (Ground ambulance is NOT covered.)

4. **Good faith estimates for uninsured/self-pay patients**: Providers must give you a written cost estimate before scheduled services. If the final bill exceeds the estimate by $400 or more, you can dispute it.

### What It Does NOT Cover

- Elective out-of-network care you knowingly chose
- Ground ambulance services
- Services at out-of-network facilities (unless it is an emergency)
- Post-stabilization care if you consent in writing to out-of-network treatment

### How to Use Your Rights

If you receive a surprise bill that you believe violates the No Surprises Act:

1. Do not pay it immediately
2. Call your insurance company and ask them to reprocess the claim at in-network rates
3. Contact the provider's billing department and cite the No Surprises Act
4. File a complaint at [cms.gov/nosurprises](https://www.cms.gov/nosurprises) or call 1-800-985-3059
5. You can initiate an Independent Dispute Resolution (IDR) process if you are uninsured or self-pay

## What to Do With a Surprise Bill

If you receive a bill that seems unexpectedly high:

1. **Do not pay immediately.** You typically have 30 days before any collection action.
2. **Request an itemized bill** (see below).
3. **Compare the bill to your EOB.** The "patient responsibility" amounts should match.
4. **Check for errors.** Are the dates, services, and provider correct?
5. **Call your insurance company.** Ask why specific charges were denied or not covered.
6. **Call the provider's billing department.** Ask for an explanation of each charge.
7. **Invoke the No Surprises Act** if the bill involves emergency care or out-of-network providers at an in-network facility.
8. **Negotiate.** Providers will often reduce bills if you ask, especially if you are uninsured or facing financial hardship.

## Payment Plans

Most hospitals and many providers offer payment plans:

- **Interest-free plans**: Many hospitals offer 0% interest plans for 6-24 months. Always ask.
- **Minimum payments**: You can often negotiate payments as low as $25-50/month.
- **No requirement to accept their first offer**: If a hospital offers a 6-month plan, you can ask for 12 or 24 months.
- **Third-party financing**: Some providers use companies like CareCredit. Be cautious -- these often charge high interest rates (25%+) after a promotional period.
- **Key tip**: As long as you are making regular payments and communicating with the billing office, most providers will not send your account to collections.

## Medical Debt and Credit Impact

### Current Rules (as of 2025-2026)

Medical debt reporting has changed significantly in recent years:

- **Paid medical debt** no longer appears on credit reports (effective July 2022, Equifax/Experian/TransUnion voluntary policy).
- **Medical debt under $500** is no longer reported on credit reports (effective April 2023).
- **Medical collections** must wait at least **365 days** before appearing on a credit report, giving you time to resolve billing disputes or arrange payment.
- **CFPB rule (finalized 2024)**: The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau finalized a rule to remove all medical debt from credit reports and prohibit lenders from using medical debt in lending decisions. Implementation timelines may vary due to legal challenges, so check the latest status.

### What This Means for You

- A single medical bill will not immediately ruin your credit.
- You have at least a year to resolve disputes or set up payment plans before any credit impact.
- Even if medical debt reaches collections, the financial impact on your credit score is less than it used to be.
- Always dispute any medical debt on your credit report that resulted from a billing error or insurance processing issue.

## How to Dispute a Medical Bill

### Step-by-Step Process

1. **Request an itemized bill** with CPT codes for every service.
2. **Compare to your EOB.** Do the charges, dates, and services match?
3. **Check for errors** (see common errors below).
4. **Call the billing department.** Explain what you believe is incorrect. Document the date, time, and name of the person you spoke with.
5. **File a written dispute.** Send a letter (email or certified mail) specifying the charges you are disputing and why. Request a response within 30 days.
6. **Appeal with your insurance company** if a claim was wrongly denied. You have the right to an internal appeal and, if that fails, an external review by an independent third party.
7. **File complaints** with your state insurance commissioner or the federal No Surprises Act hotline if needed.
8. **Consider a patient advocate.** Hospital patient advocates are free. Independent medical billing advocates charge fees but can negotiate significant reductions.

## Common Billing Errors to Look For

Medical billing errors are surprisingly common. Studies estimate 30-80% of medical bills contain at least one error. Check for:

| Error Type | What to Look For |
|---|---|
| **Duplicate charges** | Same service listed twice on the same date |
| **Unbundling** | Services that should be billed as one code are split into multiple higher-cost codes |
| **Upcoding** | A more expensive service code is used than what was actually performed (e.g., billed for a Level 5 ER visit when you had a Level 3 issue) |
| **Wrong patient info** | Name, DOB, or insurance ID is incorrect, causing claim denial |
| **Services not received** | Charges for procedures, tests, or medications you did not receive |
| **Incorrect date of service** | Wrong date can cause insurance to deny the claim |
| **Operating room time errors** | Billed for more time than your surgery actually took |
| **Wrong diagnosis code** | An incorrect ICD-10 code can cause a denial or higher charge |
| **Balance billing by in-network providers** | Illegal -- you should never owe more than your cost-sharing for in-network care |

## Facility Fees: Why ER Bills Are So High

A **facility fee** is a separate charge hospitals add for using their building, equipment, nursing staff, and infrastructure -- on top of the doctor's fee for the actual medical care.

### How It Works

When you visit an ER, you typically receive at least two bills:

1. **Physician fee**: The doctor's charge for examining and treating you
2. **Facility fee**: The hospital's charge for the ER room, nurses, monitoring equipment, supplies, and overhead

The facility fee is often the larger of the two and can range from $500 to $3,000+ depending on the severity level assigned to your visit (coded as ER Levels 1-5).

### Why This Matters

- Hospital-owned clinics and urgent cares can also charge facility fees, even though they look like regular doctor's offices.
- The same doctor visit at a hospital-owned clinic may cost 2-3x more than at an independent clinic because of the facility fee.
- **Ask before you go**: "Does this location charge a facility fee?" This one question can save hundreds of dollars.

## How to Request an Itemized Bill

You have the legal right to an itemized bill. Here is how to get one:

1. **Call the billing department** listed on your bill or statement.
2. **Say**: "I would like an itemized bill with CPT codes and descriptions for all charges."
3. **Specify**: You want the detailed version, not just a summary. A proper itemized bill shows each individual service, supply, and medication with its code and unit price.
4. **Follow up in writing** if they do not provide it within 7-10 business days.
5. **For uninsured/self-pay patients**: Under the No Surprises Act, you are entitled to a Good Faith Estimate before scheduled care and can dispute charges that exceed it by $400+.

**Tip:** Always request the itemized bill before paying. It is much harder to get a refund for billing errors after you have already paid.

## Financial Assistance / Charity Care Programs

### Hospital Charity Care

All nonprofit hospitals (the majority of US hospitals) are **required by federal law** (IRS Section 501(r)) to have a Financial Assistance Policy (FAP). This means:

- They must offer free or reduced-cost care to patients who qualify based on income.
- They must publicize the program (though many do a poor job of this).
- Eligibility varies but often covers patients earning up to 200-400% of the Federal Poverty Level (FPL). For a single person in 2025-2026, 200% FPL is roughly $30,000-31,000/year.
- **You can apply even after receiving a bill.** There is no requirement to apply before treatment.

### How to Apply

1. Ask the hospital billing department for a **Financial Assistance Application**.
2. You will need to provide proof of income (pay stubs, tax return, bank statements).
3. Processing takes 2-6 weeks. Bills are typically put on hold while your application is reviewed.
4. If approved, charges can be reduced by 50-100%.

### Other Programs

- **Medicaid**: If your income is very low, you may qualify for Medicaid even retroactively (up to 3 months before your application date in most states).
- **Hospital payment assistance offices**: Many hospitals have dedicated staff to help you find programs.
- **Nonprofit organizations**: Groups like the Patient Advocate Foundation, Dollar For, and RIP Medical Debt help patients reduce or eliminate medical bills.
- **State programs**: Many states have additional protections and assistance programs. Check with your state's health department.

## Timeline: When to Expect a Bill After a Visit

| Event | Typical Timeline |
|---|---|
| Visit / treatment occurs | Day 0 |
| Provider submits claim to insurance | 1-7 days after visit |
| Insurance processes the claim | 2-6 weeks after claim submission |
| EOB arrives (mail or online) | 2-6 weeks after visit |
| Medical bill arrives from provider | 4-8 weeks after visit |
| Second bill / reminder if unpaid | 30 days after first bill |
| Account may go to collections | 90-180 days after first bill (varies by provider) |

**Notes:**
- ER visits often generate multiple bills from different providers (hospital, physician group, radiologist, lab) that arrive at different times over several weeks.
- Always wait for your EOB before paying any bill.
- Some providers now post bills to online patient portals before mailing paper copies.

## Worked Example: Typical ER Visit Flow

**Scenario:** You twist your ankle badly and go to the ER. You have insurance with a $1,500 deductible (not yet met), 20% coinsurance, and $250 ER copay.

### Step 1: The Visit (Day 0)

You are seen by an ER doctor, get an X-ray, and receive a splint. You show your insurance card at check-in and pay nothing at the time of service.

### Step 2: Behind the Scenes (Days 1-14)

The hospital submits claims to your insurance for:

| Service | Billed Amount |
|---|---|
| ER Facility Fee (Level 3) | $2,800 |
| ER Physician Fee | $750 |
| X-Ray (Ankle, 2 views) | $450 |
| Splint + Supplies | $200 |
| **Total Billed** | **$4,200** |

### Step 3: EOB Arrives (Week 3)

Your insurance processes the claim and sends you an EOB:

| Service | Billed | Allowed | Plan Paid | You Owe |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ER Facility Fee | $2,800 | $1,400 | $0* | $1,400 |
| ER Physician Fee | $750 | $400 | $0* | $400 |
| X-Ray | $450 | $250 | $110 | $140 |
| Splint + Supplies | $200 | $120 | $64 | $56 |
| **Totals** | **$4,200** | **$2,170** | **$174** | **$1,996** |

*Why did the plan pay $0 for the first two items?* Your $1,500 deductible had not been met. The first $1,500 of allowed charges comes entirely out of your pocket. After the deductible is met, you pay 20% coinsurance.

**Breakdown of your $1,996:**
- Deductible applied: $1,500 (you pay 100% of the first $1,500 in allowed charges)
- Coinsurance on remaining $670 of allowed charges: $670 x 20% = $134
- ER Copay: may or may not apply in addition (varies by plan; some plans waive copay when deductible applies)
- Provider write-off: $2,030 ($4,200 - $2,170) -- you never pay this

### Step 4: Bills Arrive (Weeks 4-8)

You receive two separate bills:
- **Hospital bill**: $1,596 (facility fee + X-ray + supplies, minus insurance payments)
- **Physician group bill**: $400 (ER doctor fee)

### Step 5: What to Do

1. **Compare both bills to the EOB.** Do the "you owe" amounts match?
2. **Request itemized bills** if the line items are not clear.
3. **Check for errors.** Were you charged for the correct ER level? Is the X-ray count right?
4. **Call the hospital billing department** to ask about payment plans. Request a 12-month interest-free plan.
5. **Apply for financial assistance** if the total is a hardship.
6. **Pay on the plan.** $1,996 over 12 months = ~$166/month.

## Quick Reference: Your Rights

- You have the right to an **itemized bill** for any medical service.
- You have the right to a **Good Faith Estimate** before scheduled services (if uninsured/self-pay).
- You are protected from **surprise balance billing** for emergency care and out-of-network providers at in-network facilities (No Surprises Act).
- You have the right to **appeal** any insurance denial (internal appeal + external review).
- You have the right to apply for **financial assistance** at any nonprofit hospital.
- Medical debt under $500 does **not appear** on credit reports.
- Paid medical debt does **not appear** on credit reports.
- Medical debt must wait **365 days** before it can be reported to credit bureaus.
