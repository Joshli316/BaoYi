# Health Insurance Terms International Students Misunderstand

> **Why this matters:** [Research shows](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10588627/) that 57% of international students feel confused about health insurance, and roughly 80% cannot determine their financial responsibilities in medical settings. This guide explains 11 critical terms with plain-English definitions, worked numerical examples, and common misconceptions.

---

## How U.S. Health Insurance Costs Work (Overview)

Before diving into individual terms, here is how the major cost-sharing pieces fit together in a typical plan year:

| Step | What Happens | Who Pays |
|------|-------------|----------|
| 1. You pay your **premium** each month | Keeps your plan active | You |
| 2. You pay 100% of bills until you hit your **deductible** | First $500--$2,000+ of care per year | You |
| 3. After the deductible, you split costs via **copays** and **coinsurance** | A fixed fee or percentage of each bill | You + insurer |
| 4. Once your spending hits the **out-of-pocket maximum**, insurance pays 100% | No more cost-sharing for covered services | Insurer |

Source: [HealthCare.gov -- Your Total Costs](https://www.healthcare.gov/choose-a-plan/your-total-costs/)

---

## 1. Deductible

**Plain-English definition:** The amount you must pay out of your own pocket each plan year before your insurance starts sharing costs. Think of it as an annual "entry fee" before your coverage kicks in.

**Worked example:**
Your plan has a **$1,500 annual deductible**. In February, you visit urgent care and the bill is $400. In April, you get lab work for $600. In July, you break your wrist and the ER bill is $3,000.

| Visit | Billed | You Pay | Deductible Used So Far | Insurance Pays |
|-------|--------|---------|----------------------|----------------|
| Urgent care (Feb) | $400 | $400 | $400 / $1,500 | $0 |
| Lab work (Apr) | $600 | $600 | $1,000 / $1,500 | $0 |
| ER visit (Jul) | $3,000 | $500 (remaining deductible) + coinsurance on the rest | $1,500 / $1,500 (met!) | Starts paying its share |

**Key details:**
- **Annual reset:** Your deductible resets to $0 on January 1 (or your plan's renewal date). Spending from last year does not carry over.
- **Individual vs. family:** A family plan has both an individual deductible (e.g., $1,500 per person) and a family deductible (e.g., $3,000 total). Once any one member hits the individual amount, that member's deductible is met. Once the family total is met, all members' deductibles are satisfied.
- **What counts:** Most medical services count. **Preventive care** (annual checkups, flu shots, screenings) usually does NOT require meeting the deductible first -- it is covered at no cost.

**Common misconceptions:**
- "I paid my deductible, so everything is free now." -- No. After the deductible, you still pay copays or coinsurance until you hit the out-of-pocket maximum.
- "My deductible carries over to next year." -- It resets every plan year.
- "Every type of visit counts toward the deductible." -- Preventive care and copay-only services often do not.

---

## 2. Copay vs. Coinsurance

**Plain-English definitions:**
- **Copay (copayment):** A **fixed dollar amount** you pay at the time of service. Example: $25 per doctor visit, $15 per generic prescription.
- **Coinsurance:** A **percentage** of the total bill you pay after meeting your deductible. Example: you pay 20%, insurance pays 80%.

Source: [HealthCare.gov -- Copayment](https://www.healthcare.gov/glossary/co-payment/) | [HealthCare.gov -- Coinsurance](https://www.healthcare.gov/glossary/co-insurance/)

**Worked example:**
You have a plan with a $1,000 deductible (already met), a $30 copay for office visits, and 20% coinsurance for hospital stays.

| Service | Total Bill | Your Cost | How It Works |
|---------|-----------|-----------|-------------|
| Doctor visit | $200 | **$30** (copay) | Fixed fee regardless of the bill total |
| Hospital stay | $10,000 | **$2,000** (coinsurance) | 20% of $10,000; insurance pays $8,000 |
| Prescription (generic) | $50 | **$10** (copay) | Fixed fee at the pharmacy |

**When each applies:**
- **Copays** typically apply to routine, predictable services: office visits, urgent care, prescriptions.
- **Coinsurance** typically applies to larger, less predictable costs: hospital stays, surgeries, imaging (MRI/CT scans).

**Common misconceptions:**
- "Copay and coinsurance are the same thing." -- Copay is a flat fee; coinsurance is a percentage. A 20% coinsurance on a $50,000 surgery is $10,000, far more than any copay.
- "I only pay the copay and nothing else." -- For services that use coinsurance, you pay a percentage, not a flat fee.
- "Copays count toward my deductible." -- In many plans, copays do NOT count toward the deductible (but they usually DO count toward the out-of-pocket maximum).

## 3. Out-of-Pocket Maximum (OOP Max)

**Plain-English definition:** The absolute most you will pay for covered services in a plan year. Once you hit this ceiling, insurance pays 100% for the rest of the year. For 2025 Marketplace plans, the federal limit is **$9,200 for an individual** and **$18,400 for a family**.

Source: [HealthCare.gov -- Out-of-Pocket Maximum](https://www.healthcare.gov/glossary/out-of-pocket-maximum-limit/)

**Worked example:**
Your plan has a $9,200 OOP max. By October, between your deductible ($1,500), copays ($700), and coinsurance ($7,000), you have paid $9,200 total. In November, you need a $15,000 surgery.

| | Amount |
|---|--------|
| Your total paid so far (deductible + copays + coinsurance) | $9,200 |
| OOP max reached? | Yes |
| November surgery ($15,000) -- you pay | **$0** |
| Insurance pays for the surgery | **$15,000** |

**What counts toward the OOP max:** Deductibles, copays, and coinsurance for in-network covered services.

**What does NOT count:** Monthly premiums, out-of-network charges (in most plans), and costs for non-covered services.

**Common misconceptions:**
- "OOP max includes my monthly premium." -- Premiums never count.
- "After hitting the OOP max, everything is free forever." -- It resets each plan year, just like the deductible.
- "Out-of-network spending counts toward my OOP max." -- Usually it does not; many plans have a separate (higher) OOP max for out-of-network care, or none at all.

---

## 4. Premium

**Plain-English definition:** The monthly fee you pay to keep your insurance plan active -- whether or not you use any medical services. Think of it like a subscription.

**Worked example:**

| Scenario | Monthly Premium | Annual Premium | Actual Medical Bills | Total Spent |
|----------|----------------|---------------|---------------------|-------------|
| Healthy year (no visits) | $350 | $4,200 | $0 | **$4,200** |
| Moderate use (a few visits) | $350 | $4,200 | $800 in copays | **$5,000** |
| Major surgery year | $350 | $4,200 | $9,200 (hit OOP max) | **$13,400** |

**Common misconceptions:**
- "If I pay a higher premium, I won't pay anything else." -- Even expensive plans have deductibles, copays, or coinsurance.
- "I can skip premium payments when I'm healthy." -- If you stop paying, your coverage is cancelled.
- "Premium = total cost of insurance." -- Premium is just the subscription fee. Your total cost is premium + deductible + copays + coinsurance.

## 5. In-Network vs. Out-of-Network

**Plain-English definition:**
- **In-network:** Doctors, hospitals, and labs that have a contract with your insurance company. They have agreed to charge pre-negotiated, lower rates.
- **Out-of-network:** Providers with no contract. They can charge whatever they want, and your insurance covers less (or nothing).

**Worked example:**

| | In-Network Surgeon | Out-of-Network Surgeon |
|---|---|---|
| Surgeon's charge | $10,000 | $15,000 |
| Insurance-negotiated rate | $6,000 | N/A |
| Insurance pays (80% coinsurance) | $4,800 | $3,000 (50% of their "allowed amount" of $6,000) |
| You pay | **$1,200** | **$12,000** (your coinsurance + the $9,000 difference) |

**Balance billing risk:** The out-of-network surgeon can bill you for the difference between their charge ($15,000) and what insurance considers reasonable ($6,000). That $9,000 gap is called a **balance bill** -- and it is legal in many situations (see Section 10 below for protections).

**Common misconceptions:**
- "Any doctor will work with my insurance." -- If they are out-of-network, your costs can be 3--10x higher.
- "The hospital is in-network, so every doctor there is too." -- An anesthesiologist or radiologist at an in-network hospital can be out-of-network.
- "I can't see an out-of-network doctor at all." -- You can, but you will pay much more.

## 6. EOB (Explanation of Benefits)

**Plain-English definition:** A document your insurance company sends you **after** processing a claim. It shows what was billed, what insurance paid, and what you may owe. **An EOB is NOT a bill.** Do not pay it. Wait for the actual bill from your doctor or hospital.

**How to read an EOB:**

| EOB Field | What It Means |
|-----------|--------------|
| Service / procedure | What was done (e.g., "Office visit, Level 3") |
| Provider's charge | The full amount the provider billed |
| Allowed amount | The negotiated rate your insurance accepts |
| Insurance paid | What your insurer actually paid the provider |
| Your responsibility | What you owe (deductible, copay, or coinsurance) |
| Claim status | Approved, denied, or pending |

**Common misconceptions:**
- "The EOB is my bill -- I need to pay it immediately." -- No. Wait for the provider's bill. The EOB is an informational statement.
- "If the EOB says I owe $0, I'm done." -- Usually yes, but check that the claim was fully processed and not still pending.
- "I can ignore EOBs." -- Always review them. Errors happen. Compare the EOB to the provider's bill to catch overcharges or services you never received.

## 7. Prior Authorization

**Plain-English definition:** A requirement that your doctor gets approval from your insurance company **before** performing certain procedures, tests, or prescribing certain medications. Without prior authorization, the insurance company may refuse to pay.

**Worked example:**
Your doctor recommends an MRI ($2,500). Your plan requires prior authorization for imaging.

| Scenario | What Happens | You Pay |
|----------|-------------|---------|
| Doctor gets prior auth, MRI approved | Insurance covers it normally | Your coinsurance (e.g., $500) |
| Doctor skips prior auth | Insurance denies the claim | **$2,500** (the full cost) |
| Prior auth denied, doctor appeals and wins | Insurance covers it after appeal | Your coinsurance (e.g., $500) |

**Common misconceptions:**
- "If my doctor orders it, insurance automatically pays." -- Many services require pre-approval. Always ask.
- "A denied prior authorization means I can't get the procedure." -- You can appeal. Your doctor can submit additional medical justification.
- "Prior authorization is a one-time thing." -- Some ongoing treatments (physical therapy, certain medications) need re-authorization periodically.

---

## 8. Formulary

**Plain-English definition:** Your insurance plan's list of approved prescription drugs, organized into **tiers** from cheapest to most expensive. If a drug is not on the formulary, your plan may not cover it at all.

**Typical tier structure:**

| Tier | Drug Type | Typical Copay | Example |
|------|----------|--------------|---------|
| Tier 1 | Generic (preferred) | $5--$15 | Ibuprofen, metformin |
| Tier 2 | Preferred brand-name | $25--$50 | Certain brand inhalers |
| Tier 3 | Non-preferred brand-name | $50--$100 | Newer brand drugs |
| Tier 4 | Specialty | 20--33% coinsurance | Biologics, cancer drugs |

**Step therapy:** Some plans require you to try a cheaper drug first (Tier 1) before they will cover a more expensive one (Tier 3). If the cheaper drug does not work, your doctor can request an exception.

**Common misconceptions:**
- "All drugs cost the same with insurance." -- Costs vary dramatically by tier.
- "My doctor's prescription guarantees coverage." -- If the drug is not on the formulary, you may pay full price.
- "Generic drugs are lower quality." -- Generics have the same active ingredients and FDA standards as brand-name drugs. They are simply cheaper because the patent has expired.

---

## 9. Referral

**Plain-English definition:** A written order from your primary care doctor (PCP) that allows you to see a specialist. Some plans (especially HMOs) require referrals; others (PPOs) do not.

**Worked example:**
You have knee pain and want to see an orthopedic specialist.

| Plan Type | Referral Needed? | What Happens Without One |
|-----------|-----------------|-------------------------|
| HMO | Yes | Insurance may deny the claim entirely |
| PPO | No | You can go directly to any in-network specialist |
| EPO | Sometimes | Check your specific plan |

**Common misconceptions:**
- "I can just book any specialist directly." -- With an HMO, you must get a referral from your PCP first.
- "A referral and prior authorization are the same thing." -- A referral directs you to a specialist; prior authorization approves a specific procedure or treatment.
- "Referrals last forever." -- Most referrals are valid for a specific time period (e.g., 90 days) or a set number of visits.

## 10. Balance Billing and Surprise Billing

**Plain-English definition:**
- **Balance billing:** When an out-of-network provider bills you for the difference between their charge and what your insurance paid. Example: the provider charges $5,000, insurance pays $3,000, and the provider sends you a bill for the remaining $2,000.
- **Surprise billing:** When you receive balance billing from an out-of-network provider you did not choose -- typically in an emergency, or when an out-of-network doctor treats you at an in-network facility.

**The No Surprises Act (effective January 2022)** protects you from surprise bills in these situations:
- **Emergency services** -- you pay only in-network cost-sharing rates, regardless of the provider's network status.
- **Non-emergency services at in-network facilities** -- if an out-of-network provider treats you (e.g., anesthesiologist, radiologist) without your consent, they cannot balance bill you.
- **Air ambulance** services from out-of-network providers.

Source: [CMS -- No Surprises Act Fact Sheet](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/nosurpriseactfactsheet-health-insurance-terms-you-should-know508c.pdf)

**Worked example:**

| Scenario | Before No Surprises Act | After No Surprises Act |
|----------|------------------------|----------------------|
| ER visit, out-of-network doctor | You could get a $5,000+ balance bill | You pay only your in-network copay/coinsurance |
| In-network hospital, out-of-network anesthesiologist | Anesthesiologist bills you the gap | You pay only in-network rates |
| You knowingly choose an out-of-network provider | Balance billing allowed | Balance billing still allowed (you signed consent) |

**Common misconceptions:**
- "The No Surprises Act means I never get a surprise bill." -- It only applies to specific situations (emergencies, non-consented out-of-network care at in-network facilities). If you knowingly choose an out-of-network provider and sign a consent form, you can still be balance billed.
- "International students are not covered." -- The No Surprises Act applies to all patients with private insurance, regardless of immigration status.

## 11. Coordination of Benefits (COB)

**Plain-English definition:** Rules that determine which plan pays first when you are covered by two insurance plans (e.g., your university plan and a spouse's employer plan). One plan is designated **primary** (pays first), and the other is **secondary** (picks up remaining costs).

**Worked example:**
You have your university student health plan (primary) and are also covered under your spouse's plan (secondary). You have a $2,000 medical bill.

| Step | What Happens | Amount |
|------|-------------|--------|
| 1. Primary plan processes the claim | Pays $1,400 (after your $200 copay and coinsurance) | $1,400 |
| 2. Remaining balance | $2,000 - $1,400 = $600 | $600 |
| 3. Secondary plan processes the remainder | Pays $500 of the remaining $600 | $500 |
| 4. You pay the rest | | **$100** |

**How primary vs. secondary is determined:**
- Your own plan is usually primary; a plan covering you as a dependent is secondary.
- For children, the "birthday rule" applies: the parent whose birthday comes first in the calendar year has the primary plan.

**Common misconceptions:**
- "Having two plans means everything is free." -- The combined coverage often reduces your costs significantly, but rarely to zero.
- "I can choose which plan is primary." -- COB rules (not personal preference) determine the order.
- "I don't need to tell either insurer about the other plan." -- You must report dual coverage. Failing to do so can result in claims being denied or payments being recouped.

## Quick Reference Card

| Term | One-Sentence Definition |
|------|------------------------|
| **Deductible** | Annual amount you pay before insurance starts sharing costs |
| **Copay** | Fixed dollar fee per visit or prescription |
| **Coinsurance** | Percentage of a bill you pay after the deductible |
| **OOP Maximum** | Yearly spending cap; after this, insurance pays 100% |
| **Premium** | Monthly subscription fee to keep your plan active |
| **In-Network** | Providers with negotiated lower rates through your insurer |
| **Out-of-Network** | Providers without a contract; you pay much more |
| **EOB** | Statement from insurer showing what was billed/paid -- NOT a bill |
| **Prior Authorization** | Pre-approval from insurer before certain treatments |
| **Formulary** | Insurer's tiered list of covered prescription drugs |
| **Referral** | PCP's written order to see a specialist |
| **Balance Billing** | Provider billing you the gap between their charge and insurance payment |
| **COB** | Rules for which plan pays first when you have two plans |
